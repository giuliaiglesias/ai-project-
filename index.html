01 / UNDERSTAND

So, what actually is AI?

Artificial intelligence is software that uses inputs to produce things like predictions, recommendations or content. Think of a spam filter sorting messages or a chatbot answering a question. The OECD’s definition includes systems with different levels of independence.

Many AI systems learn patterns from examples instead of following only rules written by people. Generative AI uses learned patterns to produce new text, images or audio. A language model predicts pieces of text in sequence; a convincing answer can still be wrong. [2]

02 / WEIGH IT UP

Helpful doesn’t mean harmless.

These everyday examples show why the way we use AI matters.

A / LEARNING

A study partner

AI could explain a difficult topic in simpler words or suggest practice questions. I would use it to get started, then compare the explanation with class notes.

The catch

A fluent answer may invent facts or references. Checking the original source matters. [2]

B / WORK

A first draft

AI could help turn rough notes into an email or suggest code. This leaves more time for choosing the right message and testing the result.

The catch

Sensitive inputs can create privacy risks; outputs can reproduce bias. Review what goes in and comes out. [2]

C / COMMUNICATION

More ways to connect

Translation and captions can help people access information across languages. Accuracy still needs checking, especially when the meaning is important.

The catch

Synthetic voices and videos can support impersonation and fraud. Verify unusual requests through a separate, trusted channel. [2]

A quick thought experiment +

An AI-generated message sounds like your group partner asking for your account password. Would a familiar voice be enough proof? My response would be to contact them separately and keep the password private.

03 / LOOK AHEAD

The cloud has a power cable.

AI runs on physical computers. The IEA’s 2025 report estimated worldwide data-centre electricity use at 415 TWh in 2024 and projected about 945 TWh in 2030. This includes all data centres, not only AI. [3]

The IEA warns that grid constraints could delay projects. This is a reason to plan electricity supply carefully, rather than proof that AI will cause blackouts. [3]

POSSIBLE FUTURE

More useful, more connected

AI could support research, accessible services and everyday tasks. But cloud outages could interrupt work that depends on it. My view: keep backups and a way to continue offline.

MY VIEW

Keep people in charge

AI can also assist phishing and cyberattacks. [2] More autonomous tools should have limited permissions, testing and human approval before important actions. A more capable system needs meaningful supervision.
